# Bubble Files 01 · Two Texas Brothers Tried to Corner the World's Silver

**Length:** about 10 minutes (around 1,500 spoken words at the faster Fable pace)
**Format:** 16:9 long-form. Mixed style: full-screen archive footage, map moves, the evidence board for the complicated bits, and the brothers' photos (fair use).
**Ad:** 15 seconds at about 5:00, right at the cliffhanger, with a green progress bar along the bottom and the 101 dot narrator.

Visual notes are in *italics*. Only the plain text is read aloud.

## Cold open · 0:00

*Full screen: molten silver in slow motion. The price counter ticks up in the corner.*

Okay, so picture this. It's January 1980, and silver just hit fifty dollars an ounce. A year ago it was six bucks. And people are going into their kitchens, pulling out grandma's forks and tea sets, and selling them so somebody can melt them down.

So who's buying all of it? Two brothers from Texas. And by the end of this story, they're going to lose more than a billion dollars of it in a matter of weeks.

*Title card: BUBBLE FILES · The Hunt Brothers. Music drops out for a beat.*

## 1 · The Hunts · 0:40

*1950s oil field footage. Pull out to a map, East Texas fills in.*

To get why this happened, you kind of have to start with their dad, H. L. Hunt. Back in 1930 he bought the rights to an oil well in East Texas from a wildcatter everybody called Dad Joiner, and that well turned out to sit on one of the biggest oil fields ever found in America. So by the fifties, H. L. was being called one of the richest men in the world.

*Photos of Bunker and Herbert drop onto the Texas map.*

He had a lot of kids, but two of them matter for our story. Nelson Bunker Hunt, who everyone just called Bunker, and his younger brother, William Herbert. Bunker's the big personality here. He loved racehorses, and he'd made his own fortune finding oil in Libya, out in the Sahara, in a field called Sarir.

*Map slides east to Libya. The field gets a red circle, then a stamp: NATIONALIZED · 1973.*

And then in 1973, Libya's government under Muammar Gaddafi just took it. Nationalized the whole thing.

So imagine you're Bunker. A government just walked in and took your oil field. Back home, inflation is eating the dollar, and a couple of years earlier Nixon had cut the dollar loose from gold. So you start thinking, okay, what can't they print, and what can't they take?

## 2 · Why silver · 2:15

*Evidence board: a 1933 gold order, a card that reads "GOLD: ILLEGAL", a silver bar.*

Gold would've been the obvious answer, except for one problem. Americans weren't allowed to own gold bullion. It had basically been illegal since 1933, and it stayed that way until the very end of 1974.

Silver, though? Totally legal. And cheap. When the Hunts started buying in 1973, silver was around two dollars an ounce.

*Archive: a jet on a runway. A map line draws from Texas to Switzerland.*

So they bought. A lot. And they didn't want paper promises, they wanted the actual metal. They reportedly chartered jets to fly tens of millions of ounces to vaults in Switzerland, with a team of armed cowboys riding along as guards.

By early 1974, the two brothers were holding around fifty million ounces. And they were just getting started.

## 3 · The squeeze · 3:20

*Archive trading floor, full screen. A calendar flips to 1979.*

Now fast forward to 1979. Inflation in America is in double digits and everyone's nervous about money. The Hunts team up with some wealthy investors from Saudi Arabia, and together they start buying silver again, both the metal and futures contracts, which are basically promises to receive silver later.

*Board: a futures contract card, an arrow to a pile of real bars.*

And here's the trick. When those contracts came due, most traders just settled in cash. The Hunts asked for the actual silver. So the market had to find real metal to hand over, and there just wasn't that much of it lying around.

*The red price chart draws itself: $6, $10, $20, $34.45.*

So the price started to climb. Six dollars. Ten. Twenty. By the last day of 1979 it's thirty-four dollars. With their partners, the Hunts are sitting on almost two hundred million ounces, and on paper they're up billions.

*Chart punches past $50. Hold.*

And in January 1980, it goes past fifty.

That's when the grandma's forks thing happens. Families are selling silverware, coins, tea sets, anything that's silver, to dealers who melt it all down.

So it's 1980 and two brothers basically own the silver market. Could anyone pull something like this off today?

## 4 · The rules change · 5:20

*Back to the archive floor. The COMEX stamp: LIQUIDATION ONLY.*

So here's the problem with cornering a market. The people who run the market get to change the rules.

In January 1980, the COMEX, which is the exchange in New York where silver futures traded, put limits on how many contracts any one trader could hold. Then on January twenty-first it went further. From now on you could only trade to close out your positions. No new buying.

And think about that. The Hunts' whole strategy was buying. And overnight, buying just wasn't allowed.

*Chart: a sharp drop.*

The price cracked almost right away. On January twenty-second, silver fell about ten dollars in a single day.

*Archive or photo: Paul Volcker. A line chart of interest rates going up.*

At the same time, the new chairman of the Federal Reserve, Paul Volcker, was pushing interest rates way up to fight inflation and leaning on banks to stop lending money to people speculating on metals.

And that mattered, because the Hunts hadn't paid for all this silver with cash. They'd borrowed. A lot.

## 5 · Silver Thursday · 7:00

*Board: a "MARGIN CALL" card. A phone rings.*

Here's how borrowing works in a trade like this. When the price goes down, your broker calls and asks for more money to cover the loss. That's called a margin call. And through February and March, the calls kept coming, and the Hunts kept paying, until they couldn't.

*The newspaper ad graphic. The word lands: Unconscionable.*

On March twenty-sixth, Tiffany's, yeah, that Tiffany's, runs an ad in the New York Times calling the whole thing unconscionable. And that same week, Herbert Hunt tells the brokers the family can't meet the next calls.

*Calendar flips to Thursday, March 27. The chart falls. Screen shake, the only one in the video.*

And the next day, Thursday, March twenty-seventh, 1980, silver closes at ten dollars and eighty cents. It was twenty-one dollars the day before. Half of it, gone in one day.

*Archive: TAPE DELAY board, traders looking up.*

One of their brokers, Bache, was suddenly owed about a hundred and twenty million dollars it wasn't going to get. Trading in Bache's own stock got suspended. And people started to worry this wasn't just a Hunt problem anymore, that it could pull banks down with it.

*Title: SILVER THURSDAY.*

That day got a name. Silver Thursday.

## 6 · Aftermath · 8:30

*Board: a bank loan document, a stamp: $1.1 BILLION.*

In the end, a group of banks put together a loan of around a billion dollars to the family's oil company, Placid Oil, so the Hunts could pay what they owed and stop the dominoes. The Fed was fine with it, as long as none of the money went back into silver.

*Map: a line from Peru to New York. Photo of a courthouse.*

But that wasn't the end. A Peruvian minerals company called Minpeco sued them, and in 1988 a jury found the Hunts liable for conspiring to corner the silver market. That same year Bunker and Herbert filed for bankruptcy, at the time one of the biggest personal bankruptcies in American history. Bunker had to sell hundreds of his racehorses. And in 1989 they settled with regulators and were banned from trading commodities.

## 7 · So what's the lesson · 9:40

*Host shot or the board pulled all the way back, every string connected.*

So what's the lesson here? Honestly, it's not that silver is bad. The Hunts were kind of right about inflation, prices did keep going up. Their mistake was size and debt. They bet so big, with so much borrowed money, that the market and the people running it had no choice but to push back.

*The chart extends all the way to today.*

And here's a wild footnote. It took silver about forty-five years to get back above fifty dollars. That finally happened in 2025. And adjusted for inflation, the Hunts' fifty-dollar silver would be something like two hundred dollars today.

So were they geniuses who got stopped, or two guys who flew way too close to the sun? Let me know in the comments. And next time, a flower that once cost more than a house. See you there.
