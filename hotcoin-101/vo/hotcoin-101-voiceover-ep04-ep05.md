### Paste block, FILE 004

```
You hear the word constantly. Here's what it actually is.

At its core, a blockchain is a list of payments. Who sent what, to whom. Nothing physically moves anywhere.

Those entries get grouped into blocks. And every block carries a short code, a hash, calculated from the block before it.

Which is why you can't quietly edit an old one. Change it, and every hash after it stops matching.

And there's no master copy anywhere. Thousands of machines hold the same list, and they check it against each other constantly.

Next to a bank, the real difference is who holds the record. One company, or thousands of copies of it.

It's worth knowing what it doesn't do. It won't make a wrong entry true, and it won't keep you anonymous.

The first block was mined in January 2009. The core design hasn't changed since.

Cheap to check, expensive to fake. Everything else in crypto is built on that.
```

### Paste block, FILE 005

```
It's easy to think you're buying from the exchange. You're not.

Every market is two queues. Buyers waiting below the price, sellers waiting above it, each with a size attached. That's the order book.

A trade happens when those two queues touch. Someone who wants out meets someone who wants in.

The gap between the highest bid and the lowest ask is the spread. That's what it costs you not to wait.

And a trade isn't finished when it prints. Matching takes microseconds. Moving the ownership takes a lot longer.

You're not alone in there. Market makers quote both sides all day, and institutions slice big orders into small ones.

Crypto exchanges work the same way. They just never close, and you're usually hitting the book yourself.

In 1792, twenty four brokers signed an agreement under a tree. The matching problem hasn't changed since.

An exchange doesn't sell you the asset. It sells you a reliable way to find the other side.
```
