1/ The S&P 500 isn't the economy. It's five hundred opinions about it 🟢

2/ An index starts simple. A basket of companies. Except they don't all count the same, because the rule decides how much each one weighs.

3/ By number, the ten biggest are almost nothing. By weight, they do most of the moving. So when "the market" is up, quite often a handful of names are up.

4/ Then there's survivorship. The list edits itself. Companies that fail get quietly replaced, which is why a long-run chart only shows you the survivors.

5/ Take the same five hundred companies and weight them equally instead. Different line, different number, and neither version is wrong. They're answering different questions.

6/ So three questions for any index:

→ Who gets in, and who decides?
→ How is each one weighted?
→ What happens to the ones that fail?

"The market is up" is doing a lot of work in most conversations 🟢

The S&P 500 isn't the economy. It's a rule, and the rule has three parts that almost never get mentioned.

Who gets in. A committee decides, using criteria that have changed over time.

How much each one counts. It's weighted by market value, so the largest companies do most of the moving. By number, the ten biggest are a rounding error. By weight, they are not.

What happens to failures. They get dropped and replaced. Which means a long-run index chart is a chart of the survivors, and the companies that didn't make it are simply no longer in the line.

Take the same five hundred companies and weight them equally instead, and you get a visibly different result. Neither version is wrong. They are answering different questions, and only one of them is usually quoted.

So before reading the number, it's worth reading the rule, because the rule is what you're actually buying.
